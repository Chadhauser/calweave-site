Time tracking without timers: bill from the Google Calendar you already keep

Freelancers and small teams buy time-tracking apps, forget to start the timers, and end up reconstructing the week from… their calendar. Skip the middle step. If client work goes in your calendar anyway, the calendar is the timesheet — it just needs to become rows in a sheet.

Step 1 — make events self-describing

Adopt one small habit, either flavour: keyword tagging — start client-work titles with a short code (“ACME — board pack”, “SMITH — call”); or colour tagging — one calendar colour per client or per billable/non-billable. Thirty seconds of discipline at booking time replaces every timer you'd forget to start.

Step 2 — get events into a Sheet as structured rows

Export or sync your calendar to a Google Sheet so each event is a row with title, start, end and colour (see the three ways to do that — for time tracking you want the sync option, because a timesheet that's stale by Friday is no timesheet).

Step 3 — three formulas do the billing

  1. Duration: =(end-start)*24 formatted as a number = decimal hours.
  2. Client: =IFERROR(LEFT(title, FIND(" — ", title)-1), "Internal") pulls your tag; or map colours to clients with a small lookup table and VLOOKUP.
  3. Money: hours × rate from the same lookup table.

Then one pivot table — rows: client; values: sum of hours and of fees; filter: month — and month-end invoicing is reading numbers off a screen. Add a second pivot by week to see where the time actually goes; it is reliably humbling.

What breaks this system (and the fixes)

Stale data — solved by auto-sync on a schedule rather than manual exports. Duplicated rows after re-imports — solved only by ID-matched syncing, not by appending. Recurring events — make sure your sync expands each occurrence into its own row, or every weekly call counts once. Big calendars timing out in DIY scripts — the six-minute problem, explained.
